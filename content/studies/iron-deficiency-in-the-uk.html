How common is it?

The National Diet and Nutrition Survey (NDNS) for 2016/17 to 2018/19 found that 49% of girls aged 11 to 18 and 25% of women aged 19 to 64 had iron intakes below the Lower Reference Nutrient Intake (LRNI)1. The LRNI is the intake likely to be enough for only 2.5% of people, so intakes below it are considered low1.

The same survey found evidence of both iron deficiency anaemia (low haemoglobin) and low iron stores (low plasma ferritin) in 9% of girls aged 11 to 18, 5% of women aged 19 to 64 and 2% of women aged 65 and over1. In NDNS reporting, haemoglobin below 115 to 130 g/L (depending on age and sex) indicates iron deficiency anaemia, and plasma ferritin below 15 µg/L indicates low iron stores8.

For comparison, the British Society of Gastroenterology notes a point prevalence of iron deficiency anaemia of 2% to 5% among adult men and postmenopausal women in the developed world6.

Who is most at risk?

According to the NHS, heavy periods and pregnancy are very common causes of iron deficiency anaemia3. In pregnancy, it is most often caused by a lack of iron in the diet3.

Bleeding in the stomach and intestines is another common cause3. This bleeding can come from:

  • taking non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen and aspirin
  • stomach ulcers
  • inflammation of the bowel or gullet
  • piles
  • less commonly, bowel or stomach cancer3

Women aged 19 to 49 have higher iron needs: the NHS gives 14.8 mg a day, compared with 8.7 mg a day for men aged 19 and over and women aged 50 and over7.

Signs to look out for

The NHS lists these main symptoms2:

  • tiredness and lack of energy
  • shortness of breath
  • noticeable heartbeats (palpitations)
  • paler than usual skin
  • headaches

Less common symptoms include2:

  • tinnitus
  • a strange taste in food
  • itchiness
  • a sore tongue
  • hair loss
  • cravings for non-food items (pica)
  • difficulty swallowing
  • painful sores at the corners of the mouth
  • spoon-shaped nails
  • restless legs

The NHS advises seeing a GP if you think you might have iron deficiency anaemia2.

How it's diagnosed

A GP will usually ask about your lifestyle and medical history and arrange a full blood count (FBC), which checks whether your number of red blood cells is normal4. If the cause is not clear, the GP may arrange further tests, such as a stool test for hidden blood (FIT), or refer you to a specialist4.

Two specialist bodies describe the tests in more detail:

  • The British Society of Gastroenterology describes serum ferritin as "the single most useful marker" of iron deficiency anaemia6.
  • In its guideline, a ferritin below 15 µg/L indicates absent iron stores, and a level below 30 µg/L generally indicates low iron stores6.
  • It uses the World Health Organization definition of anaemia: haemoglobin below 130 g/L in men and below 120 g/L in non-pregnant women over 156.
  • The British Society for Haematology notes that "no single test can provide an accurate assessment" of all aspects of iron status9.

What the guidance says

The NHS says iron tablets are usually recommended and are taken for about six months5. Possible side effects include constipation or diarrhoea, tummy pain, heartburn, feeling sick and darker poo5. Your GP may repeat blood tests to check that your iron level is returning to normal5. The GP will also look for the underlying cause. For example, heavy periods can be treated with medicine2.

The British Society of Gastroenterology's 2021 guideline recommends the following6:

  • Starting treatment: one tablet a day of ferrous sulphate, fumarate or gluconate.
  • If tablets are not tolerated: one tablet every other day.
  • How long: continue for around three months after haemoglobin returns to normal.

The same guideline says iron given by drip or injection (parenteral iron) "should be considered when oral iron is contraindicated, ineffective or not tolerated"6. It adds that this can be considered early if tablets are unlikely to work, and may be preferable from the start in people with ongoing significant bleeding6.

The NHS also gives dietary advice. Iron-rich foods include dark-green leafy vegetables, fortified cereals and bread, meat, dried fruit and pulses5. Large amounts of tea, coffee, milk and dairy can make iron harder to absorb5. Taking 17 mg a day or less of iron supplements is unlikely to cause harm7.

Key points

  • Low iron intakes are most common in teenage girls and women of childbearing age in the UK1.
  • Heavy periods, pregnancy and bleeding from the gut are common causes3.
  • Diagnosis is by blood tests, usually a full blood count, with ferritin used to assess iron stores4, 6.
  • UK guidance starts with iron tablets5, 6. The British Society of Gastroenterology guideline says iron by drip or injection should be considered in specific situations, such as when tablets do not work, cannot be taken or are unlikely to be effective6.
