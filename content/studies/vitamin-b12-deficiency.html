How common is it?

NICE says vitamin B12 deficiency is more common in older people1. It is thought to affect around 5% of people aged 65 to 74 and more than 10% of people aged 75 and over1. The NHS gives a similar estimate for vitamin B12 and folate deficiency combined: around 1 in 10 people aged 75 or over and 1 in 20 people aged 65 to 743.

Who is most at risk?

The NHS says pernicious anaemia, an autoimmune condition that stops the body absorbing vitamin B12, is the most common cause of B12 deficiency in the UK4. NICE's 2024 guideline uses the term "autoimmune gastritis" for this condition instead of "pernicious anaemia"2.

NICE lists these risk factors1:

  • Diet: a diet low in vitamin B12, such as a vegan diet without B12 supplements or fortified foods.
  • Health conditions: atrophic gastritis, coeliac disease and other autoimmune conditions, such as thyroid disease and type 1 diabetes, or a family history of these.
  • Surgery and treatment: many types of weight-loss surgery, gastrectomy, some bowel operations, and previous abdominal or pelvic radiotherapy.
  • Medicines: including metformin, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), H2-receptor antagonists, colchicine, pregabalin, topiramate, phenobarbital and primidone.
  • Recreational use of nitrous oxide.

NICE notes that age alone is not a risk factor that would justify testing, unless there are also other risk factors, symptoms or signs1.

Signs to look out for

The NHS lists symptoms that include5:

  • feeling weak or tired
  • shortness of breath
  • headaches
  • a sore or red tongue, sometimes with mouth ulcers
  • problems with memory, understanding and judgement

B12 deficiency can also cause neurological symptoms, such as5:

  • numbness, or pins and needles
  • muscle weakness
  • problems with balance and coordination
  • psychological problems, ranging from mild depression or anxiety to confusion

NICE's list of common symptoms and signs includes "unexplained fatigue", cognitive difficulties ("brain fog"), a sore, inflamed tongue (glossitis), eyesight problems, and problems with nerves, balance or walking1. NICE says B12 deficiency should not be ruled out just because a person does not have anaemia or enlarged red blood cells1.

The NHS says it is important for B12 deficiency to be diagnosed and treated as soon as possible, because some problems it causes can be irreversible3.

How it's diagnosed

NICE recommends a blood test of either total B12 or "active B12" (holotranscobalamin), with active B12 used in pregnancy1. Blood should be taken before any B12 treatment is started1. NICE gives these levels1:

ResultTotal B12Active B12Meaning
LowBelow 180 ng/L (133 pmol/L)Below 25 pmol/LConfirmed deficiency
Middle180 to 350 ng/L25 to 70 pmol/LIndeterminate (possible deficiency)
HighAbove 350 ng/LAbove 70 pmol/LDeficiency unlikely

For people with symptoms and an indeterminate result, NICE advises considering a further test called methylmalonic acid (MMA)1. If autoimmune gastritis is suspected, NICE advises considering a test for anti-intrinsic factor antibodies, but a negative result does not rule the condition out1.

The NHS notes that some people have symptoms despite seeming to have normal B12 levels, and some have low levels without symptoms6.

What the guidance says

NICE guideline NG239 (published 6 March 2024) bases treatment on the cause1:

  • Autoimmune gastritis, or removal of the whole stomach or the end of the small bowel: lifelong B12 injections into the muscle.
  • Diet-related deficiency: B12 tablets should be considered.
  • Deficiency linked to a medicine: injections or tablets, depending on clinical judgement and the person's preference, together with a review of whether the medicine is still needed.

NICE also says:

  • Treatment should not be delayed while waiting for test results if a person has suspected megaloblastic anaemia (a type of anaemia that B12 deficiency can cause) with neurological symptoms1.
  • Symptoms may start to improve within two weeks but can take up to three months1.
  • The first follow-up is at three months, or at one month in pregnancy or breastfeeding1.

Key points

  • B12 deficiency becomes more common with age, affecting more than 1 in 10 people aged 75 and over1.
  • Common causes include autoimmune gastritis (pernicious anaemia), a diet without B12, some medicines such as metformin and PPIs, and gut surgery1, 4.
  • Symptoms can include tiredness, a sore tongue, memory problems, and nerve symptoms such as pins and needles5.
  • NICE's 2024 guideline sets out which blood tests to use and links treatment, tablets or injections, to the cause1.
