How common is it?

In the NDNS for 2019 to 2023, the proportions with a blood vitamin D level (serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, or 25(OH)D) below 25 nmol/L were1:

Age groupBelow 25 nmol/L
Adults aged 19 to 6418%
Adults aged 65 and over12%
Children aged 11 to 1823%
Children aged 4 to 1010%

The report notes that in the UK, the risk of poor musculoskeletal health is considered to increase below this level1.

Vitamin D levels follow the seasons. In every age group, levels were lowest in January to March and highest in July to September1. For people aged 11 and over, between about 21% and 38% were below 25 nmol/L in January to March, compared with 0% to 10% in July to September1.

What the guidance says

In 2016, the Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition (SACN) recommended 10 micrograms of vitamin D a day, throughout the year, for everyone aged 4 and over2. It set a "safe intake" of 8.5 to 10 micrograms a day for babies under 1, and 10 micrograms a day for children aged 1 to 42.

SACN also advised that blood levels of 25(OH)D should not fall below 25 nmol/L at any time of year3. In the UK, the skin makes vitamin D from sunlight effectively only between late March/early April and September, so dietary sources are essential in winter3.

The government advice, published by Public Health England in 2016, is that people should consider taking a daily supplement containing 10 micrograms of vitamin D in autumn and winter4, 5. The NHS says that from about late March or early April to the end of September, most people should be able to make all the vitamin D they need from sunlight5.

SACN found that results from trials of vitamin D supplements were inconsistent for outcomes other than bone and muscle health, and that the data were not enough to set recommendations for those outcomes3.

The NHS advises adults, and children aged 11 to 17, not to take more than 100 micrograms (4,000 IU) a day, as it could be harmful. The limits are lower for younger children: 50 micrograms (2,000 IU) a day for ages 1 to 10, and 25 micrograms (1,000 IU) for babies under 12 months5.

Who is most at risk?

The NHS says the Department of Health and Social Care recommends a daily supplement throughout the year for adults and children over 4 who5:

  • are not often outdoors, for example if they are frail or housebound
  • live in an institution such as a care home
  • usually wear clothes that cover most of their skin when outdoors

People with dark skin, for example from African, African-Caribbean or south Asian backgrounds, may also not make enough vitamin D from sunlight5. The 2016 Public Health England advice was that these groups should consider a supplement all year round4.

Signs to look out for

According to the NHS, a lack of vitamin D can lead to bone deformities such as rickets in children, and to bone pain caused by osteomalacia in adults5. The Royal Osteoporosis Society notes that people with osteomalacia often report bone, joint and muscle pain and may have muscle weakness, but that the symptoms of vitamin D deficiency are often vague6.

How it's tested

Vitamin D status is measured with a blood test for 25-hydroxyvitamin D1. The Royal Osteoporosis Society's clinical guideline uses these thresholds6:

  • Below 25 nmol/L: deficient
  • 25 to 50 nmol/L: may be inadequate in some people
  • Above 50 nmol/L: sufficient for almost the whole population

The same guideline recommends testing for people with musculoskeletal symptoms that could be due to vitamin D deficiency, and for people with suspected bone disease6. It does not recommend universal screening of people without symptoms6.

Key points

  • Low vitamin D levels are common in the UK, especially in late winter and early spring and among teenagers1.
  • The UK recommendation is 10 micrograms a day, and everyone should consider a supplement in autumn and winter2, 4, 5.
  • People who get little sun on their skin, and people with dark skin, may need a supplement all year4, 5.
  • A blood test is usually only needed if there are symptoms or a suspected bone condition6.
