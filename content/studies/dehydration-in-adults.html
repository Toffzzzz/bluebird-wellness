Signs to look out for

The NHS lists these symptoms of dehydration1:

  • feeling thirsty
  • dark yellow, strong-smelling pee
  • peeing less often than usual
  • feeling dizzy or light-headed
  • feeling tired
  • a dry mouth, lips and tongue
  • sunken eyes
  • headaches

UK research in care homes found that common signs, such as a dry mouth or thirst, are not reliable for spotting low-intake dehydration in older residents6. In the same research, thirst was not linked to whether residents were dehydrated5.

Who is most at risk?

The NHS says babies, children and older adults are more at risk of dehydration1. It can also happen more easily if you1:

  • have diabetes
  • have been sick or have diarrhoea
  • have been in the sun or a hot environment too long
  • have drunk too much alcohol
  • sweat a lot after exercising
  • have a high temperature
  • take medicines that make you pee more (diuretics)
  • rely on other people to get you drinks

How much fluid do adults need?

The NHS says the government recommends aiming for 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day, and that water, lower-fat milk and lower-sugar drinks, including tea and coffee, all count2. You may need more if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, in a hot environment, physically active for long periods, or ill or recovering from illness2.

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) set adequate intakes of total water of 2.0 litres a day for women and 2.5 litres a day for men3, 4. These totals include water from drinking water, all kinds of drinks and moisture in food3. The same values apply to older adults3. EFSA's figures apply only to moderate temperatures and moderate physical activity3.

Dehydration in older adults: UK data

  • Care homes: a UK study of 188 care home residents (average age 86) found that 20% were dehydrated, defined as serum osmolality above 300 mOsm/kg5. Kidney function, cognitive status and diabetes were consistently linked to dehydration5.
  • Hospital admissions: a study at a UK teaching hospital of 6,632 people aged 65 and over, admitted as medical emergencies, found that 27% were dehydrated on arrival, based on a calculated blood measure (serum osmolarity above 300 mOsm/L)7. These patients had 60% higher 30-day mortality than patients who were not dehydrated, after adjustment for other factors7.

What to do and when to get help

The NHS advises drinking fluids if you have symptoms of dehydration, starting with small sips if you feel sick1. A pharmacist can recommend oral rehydration solutions if you have vomiting or diarrhoea1.

Ask for an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 if you have1:

  • unusual tiredness
  • dizziness when standing up that does not go away
  • dark yellow pee, or are peeing less than normal
  • a fast heart rate or fast breathing

Call 999 or go to A&E if someone has1:

  • blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin
  • cold skin
  • difficulty breathing
  • confusion
  • unusual sleepiness, or is hard to wake

These can be signs of shock1.

Key points

  • Older adults, people who are unwell, and people in the heat or exercising hard are more at risk of dehydration1.
  • The NHS suggests 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day as a guide2. EFSA's total water figures (2.0 litres for women, 2.5 litres for men) include water from food3.
  • UK studies found dehydration in 20% of care home residents and 27% of older people admitted to hospital as emergencies5, 7.
  • Thirst and other common signs may not be reliable in older people5, 6.
