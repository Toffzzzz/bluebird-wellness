How common are low intakes?

The NDNS report for 2008/09 to 2011/12 found evidence of intakes below the Lower Reference Nutrient Intake (LRNI) in "a substantial proportion" of older children and adults for some minerals, particularly magnesium1. Mean magnesium intakes were below the Reference Nutrient Intake in all age groups except children under 111.

The NDNS report for 2014/15 to 2015/16 found magnesium intakes below the LRNI in2:

GroupBelow LRNI
Men aged 65 to 746%
Women aged 65 to 7411%
Men aged 75 and over22%
Women aged 75 and over27%

A peer-reviewed analysis of NDNS data from 2008/09 to 2013/14 found that 15% of men and 12% of women aged 20 to 59 had magnesium intakes from food below the LRNI3. The proportion was highest among adults in their twenties, at around 19%3.

These figures describe intake, not blood levels. NDNS reports caution that the UK reference values for magnesium are based on very limited data, so intakes below the LRNI should be interpreted carefully2, 4.

What magnesium does

Food Standards Scotland says magnesium helps turn food into energy, supports muscle and nerve function, and helps maintain healthy bones5. Under GB food law, the permitted health claims for magnesium include that it "contributes to normal muscle function", "contributes to normal functioning of the nervous system", "contributes to normal energy-yielding metabolism" and "contributes to the maintenance of normal bones"6. These claims may only be used for foods that are at least a source of magnesium6.

Adults need 300 mg a day (men) and 270 mg a day (women), and magnesium is found in foods such as spinach and other leafy greens, nuts, bread, meat, fish and dairy5. Food Standards Scotland says supplements of 400 mg or less a day are unlikely to cause harm, and that high doses taken for a short time can cause diarrhoea5.

Who is at risk of low magnesium?

Lab Tests Online UK lists these possible causes of low magnesium8:

  • low dietary intake, as seen in older or malnourished people
  • alcoholism
  • gut conditions such as Crohn's disease, and prolonged diarrhoea
  • uncontrolled diabetes
  • long-term use of some medicines, such as diuretics and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)

The UK medicines regulator (MHRA) has warned that long-term use of PPIs has been linked to low magnesium (hypomagnesaemia)9. The NHS says abnormal magnesium levels can occur in conditions that affect the kidneys or intestines7.

Signs to look out for

Symptoms of low magnesium can include muscle weakness, twitching, cramping, confusion, abnormal heart rhythms and seizures8. The MHRA lists muscle twitches, tremors, vomiting, tiredness and loss of appetite, with more serious effects including tetany (muscle spasms), delirium, convulsions, dizziness and abnormal heart rhythms9.

How it's tested

Magnesium is measured with a blood test, often alongside other tests, when someone has symptoms such as nausea, weakness, twitching, cramping or an irregular heartbeat7. For people taking PPIs long term, the MHRA advises healthcare professionals to consider measuring magnesium before treatment starts and from time to time during treatment9.

Key points

  • UK survey data show low magnesium intakes in some groups, but the UK reference values for magnesium are based on limited data1, 2, 4.
  • Intake below a reference level is not the same as a diagnosed deficiency2, 4.
  • Low magnesium is linked to some medicines, including long-term PPIs and diuretics, gut conditions and alcohol misuse8, 9.
  • Testing is done with a blood test when symptoms or medicines suggest it7, 9.
