How common is it?

In the NDNS for 2019 to 2023, 83% of women of childbearing age (16 to 49) had a red blood cell folate level below 748 nmol/L, the threshold used for a higher risk of neural tube defects1.

Fewer people had levels low enough to count as deficiency (red blood cell folate below 305 nmol/L, a threshold based on the risk of a type of anaemia called megaloblastic anaemia)1:

GroupBelow 305 nmol/L
Children aged 11 to 1812% (girls 17%, boys 7%)
Adults aged 19 to 644%
Adults aged 65 and over2%
Women aged 16 to 497%

Serum folate below 7 nmol/L, which indicates clinical deficiency, was found in1:

  • 8% of children aged 4 to 10
  • 24% of children aged 11 to 18
  • 14% of adults aged 19 to 64
  • 7% of adults aged 65 and over

Folic acid in flour

In September 2021, the UK government and devolved administrations announced that folic acid would be added to non-wholemeal wheat flour across the UK2. The government estimated this would help avoid around 200 neural tube defects each year, about 20% of the annual UK total2. Wholemeal flour and gluten-free foods are not covered2.

In England, the Bread and Flour (Amendment) (England) Regulations 2024 require 0.250 mg (250 micrograms) of folic acid per 100 g of flour from 13 December 2026, with exemptions for wholemeal flour and flour from small mills4. Scotland has made equivalent regulations with the same start date for the folic acid requirement5.

The government has said that the advice for women to take a daily 400 microgram folic acid supplement will continue after fortification2, 3.

Advice before and during pregnancy

The NHS advises taking 400 micrograms of folic acid every day from before you are pregnant until you are 12 weeks pregnant, to help prevent neural tube defects such as spina bifida6. Some women at higher risk are advised to take a higher 5 milligram dose6. This includes women who:

  • have a neural tube defect themselves, or whose baby's biological father has one, or where either has a family history of one
  • have had a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect
  • have diabetes
  • take anti-epilepsy medicine or HIV anti-retroviral medicine

Who is most at risk, and signs to look out for

The NHS says folate deficiency is more common in8:

  • people with an unbalanced or restrictive diet
  • people who regularly misuse alcohol
  • people with conditions that affect absorption, such as coeliac disease
  • people taking some medicines, including some anticonvulsants, methotrexate and sulfasalazine

The body may also need more folate in pregnancy and with some illnesses8. Folate and B12 deficiency are both more common in older people7.

The NHS says most symptoms are the same whether caused by folate or B12 deficiency9. They include9:

  • feeling weak or tired
  • shortness of breath
  • headaches
  • a sore or red tongue
  • problems with memory, understanding and judgement

How it's diagnosed and treated

Blood tests check haemoglobin, whether red blood cells are larger than normal, and the level of folate in the blood10. The NHS says folate deficiency is usually treated with folic acid tablets, most often for about four months11. Before starting folic acid, your GP will check that your B12 levels are normal, because folic acid can mask B12 deficiency11.

Key points

  • Most women of childbearing age in the UK have folate levels below the level linked to lowest neural tube defect risk1.
  • Mandatory fortification of non-wholemeal wheat flour starts on 13 December 20264.
  • The NHS advice to take 400 micrograms of folic acid a day before pregnancy and until 12 weeks remains2, 6.
  • Folate and B12 deficiency share many symptoms, and B12 should be checked before folic acid is started9, 11.
